The Coil Was Never the Problem

by Nora Valdez

I've restored about thirty pinball machines in a rented garage, and most of them came to me with a part someone had already replaced for no reason. What fixed them was tracing the fault back through the wiring before I touched anything, and keeping a notebook of every machine's first symptom. The two I couldn't fix I sold to a collector, who told me in ten minutes what I had been guessing at for a year.

Review your book

Other members can't see it until you put it on the shelf.

Chapters

“Show what you said” opens your own words behind each chapter. Only you see those.

  1. 1
    Why the New Part Didn't HelpNearly every machine I bought had a part replaced by the last owner, and the fault was still there. A flipper that stays down is usually the fuse on the coil circuit or a dirty switch, not the coil. I learned to test the switch with a meter before I ordered anything.
    Hide what you said
    A flipper that stays down is almost never the coil, so I test the switch before I order anything.
  2. 2
    Reading a Schematic by Following the WireThe manuals for machines from the 1980s are half wrong, so I stopped trusting the page and started following the wire from the symptom back to the board. It took me six machines to stop skipping the ground connections, which caused most of what I used to call random.
    Show what you said
  3. 3
    The Notebook That Caught the ConnectorI wrote down the first symptom of every machine before I touched it. The notebook is how I noticed that three machines from the same arcade had the same corroded connector, and why I started carrying spares of it in my toolbox on every pickup.
    Show what you said
  4. 4
    Selling the Ones I Couldn't FixI kept the machines I couldn't fix for a year, hoping I would learn how. Then a collector offered to take two of them for parts and tell me what was wrong with them, and what he told me about the boards was worth more to me than the money.

The Coil Was Never the Problem

by Nora Valdez

I've restored about thirty pinball machines in a rented garage, and most of them came to me with a part someone had already replaced for no reason. What fixed them was tracing the fault back through the wiring before I touched anything, and keeping a notebook of every machine's first symptom. The two I couldn't fix I sold to a collector, who told me in ten minutes what I had been guessing at for a year.
