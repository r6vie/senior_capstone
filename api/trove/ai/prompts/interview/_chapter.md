- `title`: 2–8 words in sentence case, concrete and specific to this person. It names the conversation, not a category. "Tuning a piano nobody else would touch" beats "Instrument repair". No colons-plus-subtitle, no puns that need explaining, no clichés ("Lessons learned", "My journey", "Beyond the…"). Prefer the member's own words ("Why I stopped trusting the forecast").
- `summary`: 1–2 plain sentences, at most 300 characters, in the first person, as the member telling it. It works like a line on the contents page of a good nonfiction book: specific enough to believe, and open enough that someone wants to ask the next question.
  - Give the problem, the turn and what was at stake, and the claim the member stands behind. Name that a method exists, in their words, and hold back its steps: the steps are what the call is for.
  - Never add a teaser or a rhetorical question to create curiosity. The curiosity comes from the specific thing they did.
  - Keep their voice. Their odd phrases, their shrugs and their hedges ("we did okay, I guess, for a bunch of amateurs") are what make it theirs. Don't tidy "the bike guy" into "cycling specialist".
  - Keep their people, by role: "my brother", "my boss", "the landlord". Never a last name, an employer or an exact small-town location.
  - Write the chapter, not a description of it. Never mention the reader, the book, the chapter, a conversation, a talk or a call, and never open with framing like "A reader would…", "This chapter…", "The conversation covers…" or "It comes from…". One voice throughout: no "you", no name, no third person. First person makes it easy to put words in their mouth, so every claim, feeling and lesson must be one they stated.
  - Not this, a description: "How a tutoring program got started, and what a reader would learn about recruiting volunteers."
  - Not this, the whole thing given away: "I put up twelve flyers and got one volunteer. Ask each tutor to bring one friend to a single Saturday, ask the friends to stay, and keep a sign-up sheet by the door."
  - This: "I put up twelve flyers and got one volunteer. By spring we had a waitlist, and I'd stopped asking anyone to care about tutoring."
